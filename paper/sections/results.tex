\subsection{The content--sentiment tradeoff curve}
\label{sec:res-frontier}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{frontier.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The content--sentiment tradeoff curve} of each trained
policy on the $\vNsent$-sentence test split (step $12{,}000$). Each marker is
one content floor $\tau = 100\lmb \in \{0, 5, \dots, 95\}$: the policy's
greedy prompt for that $\lmb$, scored by expected content $\mathbb{E}[c]$ and
expected sentiment $\mathbb{E}[s]$ over $N=50$ task-model samples per sentence
and averaged over sentences and task-model seeds. Crosses are $95\%$ bootstrap
intervals over sentences. $\tau$ labels mark the Huber-std curve; stacked
markers are floors at which the policy emits the same prompt. Up and to the
right is better on both objectives.}
\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} is the paper's main result: the tradeoff curve each
policy traces as its conditioning input sweeps the content floor from $0$ to
$95$. Table~\ref{tab:full} summarizes each curve. All four policies produce a
genuine curve rather than a point. Expected content rises with the floor for
every arm (Spearman $\rho \ge \vLoneRho$), and at $\tau = 0$ every arm's
rewrites are strongly positive (sentiment $76$--$93$). The arms differ in how far the curve
reaches and how much sentiment it keeps along the way.

\paragraph{\rrebel{} Huber-std has the best frontier.}
Its hypervolume is $\vHuberHV$ against $\vGentHV$ for the best GRPO arm, a
paired difference of $\vGapHuberGentHV$ points
($95\%$ CI $[\vGapHuberGentHVLo, \vGapHuberGentHVHi]$). Its mean constrained
reward is $\vHuberRew$ against $\vGentRew$, a difference of
$\vGapHuberGentRew$ ($[\vGapHuberGentRewLo, \vGapHuberGentRewHi]$). Both
intervals exclude zero by a wide margin. The gain is in sentiment
($+\vGapHuberGentSen$ points averaged over floors) at no cost in content
($+\vGapHuberGentCon$). Huber-std is also the only arm whose curve extends past
content $80$: at $\tau = 95$ it reaches $\mathbb{E}[c] = \vHuberConMax$,
whereas the other three arms saturate below $80$. The dominance is
near-uniform. If a user may randomize between two of a policy's prompts ---
which achieves any convex combination of their expected scores --- then
Huber-std's curve lies above GRPO + entropy's at \emph{every} content level
(the smallest margin is $\vDomGentMixMin$ points) and above GRPO with the
base-LM reference everywhere by at least $\vDomGbaseMixMin$ points. Without
randomization there is one exception: Huber-std has no operating point with
content between $\vDomGapLo$ and $\vDomGapHi$, because its policy switches prompt
between adjacent floors there, and GRPO + entropy has a point in that gap
(Huber-std attains at least GRPO + entropy's sentiment at $\vDomGentStepFrac\%$
of content levels).

\paragraph{\rrebel{} $\ell_1$-std: a good frontier, badly indexed.}
The two metrics disagree about $\ell_1$-std, and the disagreement is
informative. Its hypervolume ($\vLoneHV$) is \emph{higher} than GRPO +
entropy's (paired difference $+\vGapLoneGentHV$,
$[\vGapLoneGentHVLo, \vGapLoneGentHVHi]$), so it can reach better operating
points. Its mean reward ($\vLoneRew$) is \emph{lower}
($\vGapLoneGentRew$, $[\vGapLoneGentRewLo, \vGapLoneGentRewHi]$), so it
chooses them badly. The cause is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:control}: from
$\tau = 50$ upward, $\ell_1$-std emits the same copy prompt for $99$--$100\%$ of
test sentences (content $\vLoneConMax$, sentiment below $9$). It over-satisfies
floors of $50$--$75$ by $5$--$29$ points and gives up the sentiment a prompt
matched to each floor would have kept. Hypervolume scores the set of operating points a
policy can reach; the constrained reward scores whether the policy picks the
right point for the floor it is given. For a $\lmb$-conditioned policy the
second is the point of the exercise. Section~\ref{sec:res-dynamics} shows that
this is a late-training regression for this run.

\paragraph{The base-LM reference makes GRPO worse, not better.}
Anchoring GRPO's KL to the pretrained backbone gives it a genuine trust region
(Section~\ref{sec:algos-caveat}). It also produces the weakest arm by a wide
margin: hypervolume $\vGbaseHV$ and reward $\vGbaseRew$, which is
$\vGapGentGbaseRew$ points below GRPO + entropy
($[\vGapGentGbaseRewLo, \vGapGentGbaseRewHi]$). Even at $\tau = 0$ its best
rewrites score $76$ on sentiment against $91$--$93$ for the other arms. The
reason is visible in Table~\ref{tab:prompts}. The reward-optimal prompts are
lists of sentiment words and copy instructions, far from the natural text the
backbone assigns high probability to, and a KL penalty toward that backbone
holds the policy close to natural-sounding prompts that are poor at the task.
The earlier concern about GRPO was that its uniform anchor gave it no trust
region. The evidence here, at $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.04$, is that a competent
anchor is not what GRPO was missing in this problem, and that \rrebel{}'s
advantage over GRPO does not depend on the uniform-anchor confound.

\subsection{Does the policy honour the constraint?}
\label{sec:res-control}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{control.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Does the floor control the policy?} Test split, step
$12{,}000$. \textbf{Left:} expected content against the floor; the dotted line
is $\mathbb{E}[c] = \tau$, so points below it miss the floor in expectation.
\textbf{Middle:} expected sentiment. \textbf{Right:} probability that an
individual task-model sample meets the floor, the quantity the constrained
reward penalizes. Bands are $95\%$ bootstrap intervals over sentences.}
\label{fig:control}
\end{figure}

A $\lmb$-conditioned policy is only useful if the floor it is given
controls what it does. Figure~\ref{fig:control} plots, against the floor,
the three quantities that matter: the expected content the policy delivers,
the sentiment it keeps, and the per-sample probability that a rewrite meets
the floor.

\paragraph{All four policies are controllable, but coarsely.}
Expected content increases with the floor for every arm (left panel), so the
conditioning input works. It is not a smooth dial, however. Every curve is a
staircase with a few flat treads: each arm has learned a handful of prompt
``modes'' and switches between them as the floor crosses thresholds
(Section~\ref{sec:res-prompts}). Within a tread, raising the floor changes
nothing.

\paragraph{Where the constraint is met and where it is not.}
All arms meet the floor in expectation for $\tau \le 40$. Above that they part
ways. GRPO with the base-LM reference tracks the floor most tightly, staying
within $6$ points of it for $\tau = 30$--$60$ (slightly above, then slightly
below from $\tau = 45$), but at low sentiment. Huber-std's hybrid prompt holds a
tread at $\mathbb{E}[c] \approx 57$--$63$ for floors $35$--$70$, missing floors
of $60$--$80$ by $2$--$7$ points in expectation; above that it switches to a copy
prompt that reaches content $85$--$90$, meets $\tau = 85$, and misses $90$ and $95$
by $1$--$5$ points. GRPO + entropy's tread at $\approx\!63$ misses every floor from
$\tau = 65$, by up to $17$ points at the top. $\ell_1$-std meets every floor up to
$75$ (\vLoneMet{} of \vNlam{} in total, the most of any arm) only because its
single high-content mode over-satisfies them, at the sentiment cost described
above. The right panel shows the per-sample view that the constrained reward
actually optimizes: no arm keeps $P(c \ge \tau)$ above $0.9$ beyond
$\tau = 10$,
and the arms trade constraint satisfaction against sentiment differently
across the range. Section~\ref{sec:theory} anticipated this. The reward is a
per-sample penalty, a surrogate for a chance constraint, not a guarantee that
the expected content clears the floor, and with a penalty constant of only
$0.01$ a policy can profitably accept some violations in exchange for
sentiment.

\subsection{What the policies learned}
\label{sec:res-prompts}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{tables/prompts}
\caption{The greedy prompt each trained policy emits at five floors, for the
first test sentence. Prompts are input-conditioned, but at a given floor every
arm emits only a few distinct prompts across all $\vNsent$ test sentences
(Table~\ref{tab:full}), so these are representative. Prompts are decoded with
the policy's tokenizer and shown verbatim; \texttt{[RLO]} is an invisible
Unicode right-to-left-override character (U+202E), and \texttt{\textbackslash n}
a newline.}
\label{tab:prompts}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:prompts} shows what the policies actually say, and it makes
the staircases of Figure~\ref{fig:control} concrete.

\paragraph{The policies learned an interpretable prompt library.}
At $\tau = 0$, where only sentiment matters, the three arms without a base-LM
anchor independently converge on nearly the same prompt: a list of effusive
words and closing quotation marks (\texttt{Awesome!!" gorgeous." loving}),
which primes the task model to continue with praise. At high floors they
converge on \emph{copy instructions} built from words like \texttt{Example},
\texttt{Replace} and \texttt{Compare}, which make the task model reproduce the
source. In between, Huber-std finds a hybrid (\texttt{Positive praise (\% contrasts}),
and that hybrid is the tread at $\mathbb{E}[c] \approx 57$ with $46$ sentiment
that gives Huber-std its lead in the middle of the curve. $\ell_1$-std has no
such hybrid: its $\tau = 30$ prompt is already built from copy-style words
(\texttt{Results}, \texttt{outcomes}, \texttt{contrasting}), and from
$\tau = 40$ it switches to the pure copy prompt (for $57\%$ of sentences at
$\tau = 40$, and $99$--$100\%$ from $\tau = 50$).

\paragraph{Input conditioning goes unused.}
Although the policy reads the source sentence, at a given floor the
\rrebel{} policies emit a median of \vLonePrompts{} distinct prompts across
the $\vNsent$ test sentences (at most \vHuberPromptsMax), and GRPO + entropy a
median of \vGentPrompts{} (at most \vGentPromptsMax). All three have learned
what is essentially a \emph{$\lmb$-indexed library} of a few prompts, not a
per-sentence prompt. GRPO with the base-LM reference is the exception, with a
median of \vGbasePrompts{} distinct prompts (at most \vGbasePromptsMax). At
low and middle floors these are natural-text fragments (\texttt{It great Love
it!}, \texttt{I love to say}) of the kind its KL anchor favours. At high floors it has found a degenerate but effective trick: an
invisible control character followed by newlines, which is close to an empty
prompt and simply makes the task model copy the source. This per-floor count
of distinct prompts is the direct collapse measurement that the v3 campaign
lost to the filesystem purge (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}). It shows that
none of the v4 policies collapsed to a single $\lmb$-independent prompt: each
uses several prompts across the range of floors.

\subsection{Learning dynamics on the test split}
\label{sec:res-dynamics}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\IfFileExists{figures/test_learning.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{test_learning.pdf}}{\fbox{\parbox{0.8\textwidth}{\centering test-split learning curves pending}}}
\caption{\textbf{Test-split learning curves.} Each arm's checkpoints at
\vLcNsteps{} matched steps, evaluated with the full protocol (one task-model
seed; across five seeds the reward's standard deviation is \vLoneSeedSD).
\textbf{Left:} mean constrained reward. \textbf{Right:} hypervolume of the
tradeoff curve. Rings mark the checkpoint each arm's $10$-sentence
\emph{development} score would select, without reference to test data.}
\label{fig:dynamics}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics} evaluates every arm's checkpoints at \vLcNsteps{}
matched steps (\vLcFirst--\vLcLast) with the same test protocol, which shows
how the step-$12{,}000$ picture came about.

\paragraph{The gap between the families is not a late-training artifact.}
Huber-std leads GRPO + entropy at every evaluated checkpoint, by
\vLcHuberGentMin--\vLcHuberGentMax{} reward points. Its reward plateaus by
mid-training, while its hypervolume continues to grow (from \vHuberHVfirst{}
at the first checkpoint to \vHuberHV{} at the end). GRPO + entropy improves
slowly throughout (reward \vGentLcMin--\vGentLcMax, hypervolume
\vGentHVfirst{} to \vGentHV) and never approaches the \rrebel{} curves. GRPO
with the base-LM reference is flat in reward (\vGbaseLcMin--\vGbaseLcMax),
and its hypervolume shrinks late in training.

\paragraph{The $\ell_1$-std run regresses late, and the regression is in indexing.}
$\ell_1$-std peaks at step \vLonePeakStep{} with reward \vLonePeakRew,
level with Huber-std, and then loses \vLoneDrop{} points by step
$12{,}000$. It stays ahead of GRPO + entropy at \vLcLoneGentPosSteps{} of the
\vLcNsteps{} checkpoints. Its hypervolume, however, stays within
\vLoneHVmin--\vLoneHVmax{} throughout. The set of operating points it can reach
does not shrink; what degrades is \emph{which} point it assigns to each floor.
Late in training the policy spreads its copy prompt to more and more floors
(Section~\ref{sec:res-frontier}), trading sentiment for surplus content.
The training-time development-set monitor shows the same peak and decline, so
this is not a test-split artifact. We do not know how typical it is. None of
the three v3 $\ell_1$-std seeds declined on their development set
(Section~\ref{sec:seed-replication}), and we have no mechanism: the obvious
candidate, $\ell_1$'s non-vanishing gradient near the fit, is ruled out in
Section~\ref{sec:th-grpo}. We report it as a property of this run.

\paragraph{Early stopping on the development set.}
A practitioner would not ship the last checkpoint blind, and the
development-set scores logged during training allow a fair selection rule
that never touches test data: pick each arm's checkpoint with the best
development score among the evaluated steps. This selects step
\vLoneDevStep{} for $\ell_1$-std (test reward \vLoneDevRew), \vHuberDevStep{}
for Huber-std (\vHuberDevRew), \vGentDevStep{} for GRPO + entropy
(\vGentDevRew) and \vGbaseDevStep{} for the base-LM-reference arm
(\vGbaseDevRew). Under this protocol both \rrebel{} variants beat both GRPO
arms, by at least \vDevFamilyGap{} reward points, and the $\ell_1$-std
regression disappears from the comparison.

\subsection{How much the reward scale varies across contexts}
\label{sec:res-scale}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{reward_scale.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Reward scale varies by orders of magnitude across contexts}
(\rrebel{} $\ell_1$-std at step \sProbeFinal; $40$ test sources $\times$ $10$
floors, $G = 16$ prompts per group drawn by the training sampler).
\textbf{Left:} in-group reward standard deviation $\hat\sigma(x,\lmb)$ by floor
(boxes: interquartile range; whiskers: 5th--95th percentile).
\textbf{Right:} the one-window KL radius that Proposition~\ref{prop:trust}
assigns to each group, computed with the group's own empirical distribution as
the reference (so it is capped at $\log G = \sLogG$ nats, a point mass). Under
the plain target, one global $\beta$ leaves some contexts unmoved and
collapses others; under the standardized target, the radii concentrate.}
\label{fig:scale}
\end{figure}

Section~\ref{sec:th-std} argued that standardization matters when the
reward's scale varies across contexts. To measure that variation directly, we
drew groups exactly as a training step does ($G = 16$ prompts per
$(x,\lmb)$, top-$k=200$ sampling, $N=50$ task-model samples per prompt), from
\rrebel{} $\ell_1$-std checkpoints at steps \sProbeSteps, for $40$ test sources
and $10$ floors each. Figure~\ref{fig:scale} shows the result.

\paragraph{The scale spans two to three orders of magnitude.}
Across groups, the in-group standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ varies by a factor
of \sRangeQ{} between its 5th and 95th percentiles. The variation is
systematic in $\lmb$. At the final probed checkpoint the median $\hat\sigma$ is
\sSigZeroFin{} at $\tau = 0$, peaks at \sSigMaxFin{} for low-to-middle floors,
and falls to \sSigNinetyFin{} at $\tau = 90$: a \sTauRatioFin-fold spread in
median alone. The mechanism is the two-branch reward. Where most samples meet
the floor, rewards are sentiment scores spread over tens of points. Where few
do, they are penalties of size $0.01\times$ the shortfall. The contexts with the
tightest floors, where few prompts are feasible, are exactly the ones with the
smallest reward differences.

\paragraph{One global temperature cannot serve all contexts.}
Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:trust} group by group quantifies the
consequence. With the plain target $\polref e^{r/\beta}$ and our
$\beta = 0.5$, \sPlainStuck{} of groups would move less than $0.01$ nats in a
window --- no learning --- while \sPlainCollapse{} would jump to within
$0.27$ nats of a point mass. Raising $\beta$ to rescue the second set freezes more
of the first, and lowering it does the reverse. With the standardized target,
\sStdStuck{} of groups fall below $0.01$ nats and \sStdCollapse{} come near the
point-mass cap; the median radius is \sStdMedKL{} nats. This is the practical
content of Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}(ii) and Proposition~\ref{prop:trust}:
in a $\lmb$-conditioned problem, dividing by the in-group standard deviation is
what lets a single $\beta$ mean the same thing at every point on the curve.
Every \rrebel{} arm we train uses standardization, so this is a measurement of
the problem, not an ablation of the method.

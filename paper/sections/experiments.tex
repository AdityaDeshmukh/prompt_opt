\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup-exp}

\paragraph{Task and data.}
Yelp sentiment transfer in the negative-to-positive direction. Content is
BERTScore~\citep{zhang2020bertscore} against the source (\texttt{roberta-large});
sentiment is a \texttt{bert-base-uncased} classifier fine-tuned on Yelp. Both
are on a $0$--$100$ scale.

\paragraph{Models.}
The policy is a \texttt{distilgpt2} backbone with a trained MLP adaptor head
(hidden width $2048$). It reads the source sentence and the scalar $\lmb$ and
emits a fixed-length $L=5$ token prompt. The head is initialized with
Xavier-uniform weights at gain $10^{-4}$, which drives its output logits to
zero, so \textbf{the initial prompt distribution is numerically uniform over
the full $50{,}257$-token vocabulary}: the measured policy entropy at step $0$
is $10.824904$ against $\ln 50257 = 10.824905$. This matters for GRPO and is
revisited in Section~\ref{sec:algos-caveat}. The task model is
\texttt{gpt2-xl}, \textbf{frozen} and inference-only in \texttt{bfloat16},
served through vLLM with top-$k=10$ sampling. Only the adaptor is trained.

\paragraph{Training.}
$12{,}000$ steps with a batch of $B=15$ sources and $\Ggrp=16$ prompts per
source. That is $240$ scored prompt--source rows per step, each scored with
$N=50$ task-model samples. The optimizer is Adam at learning rate $10^{-4}$
with gradient-norm clipping at $10$, and $\lmb \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1)$ is
redrawn per source per step. Training prompts are sampled from the policy with
top-$k=200$. \rrebel{} uses $\beta = 0.5$ and refreshes its reference every
$150$ steps. Both GRPO arms use $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.04$ with a pinned
reference. The two $\beta$s are not comparable quantities: one is a regression
scale in Eq.~\eqref{eq:logratio}, the other a KL coefficient in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:grpo}.

\paragraph{Arms and campaigns.}
Four arms, one training seed each, all trained to $12{,}000$ steps:
\rrebel{} $\ell_1$-std, \rrebel{} Huber-std, GRPO + entropy
($\alpha_H = 0.01$, uniform anchor), and GRPO with a base-LM reference
(Section~\ref{sec:algos-caveat}). The \textbf{main campaign (v5)} trains them
with Reward~II, the floor on expected content (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward-exp}, $\nu =
5$). The \textbf{comparison campaign (v4)} trained the same four arms with
Reward~I, the floor on every sample (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}). Code,
hyperparameters, budget and scheduling are otherwise identical, so the
comparison isolates the reward. Because each campaign has a single training
seed per arm, training-seed variation comes from an earlier three-seed campaign
(v3), which used Reward~I and the same code, hyperparameters and step budget
(Section~\ref{sec:seed-replication}).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
All v4 and v5 numbers are on the \textbf{held-out Yelp test split ($500$
sentences)}, evaluated after training. We sweep $\lmb$ over the $20$-point grid
$\{0, 0.05, \dots, 0.95\}$ (every point lies inside the training range). For
each $(x,\lmb)$ the policy supplies one prompt, which is scored by averaging
$N = 50$ task-model samples, and we report expected content
$\mathbb{E}[c\mid\tau]$ and expected sentiment $\mathbb{E}[s\mid\tau]$ as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:curve}. Scorers run in \texttt{float32}.
\emph{How the prompt is chosen} differs between the campaigns. In v5 it is
\textbf{sampled from the policy}, with the training sampler (top-$k = 200$), so
that averaging over sentences estimates the expectation over prompts that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:curve} and Proposition~\ref{prop:eps} call for. In v4 it came
from the codebase's legacy decoder. That decoder is labelled greedy but in fact
samples each token from the top three, which for these peaked policies returns
the most likely prompt almost always (Section~\ref{sec:res-diagnosis}).
We repeat the final-checkpoint evaluation with \textbf{five seeds}, averaging
over seeds first and then over sentences. In v5 each seed redraws both the
prompts and the task model's samples. In v4 the prompt sampler had a fixed
seed, so prompts were identical across seeds on a given GPU model and differed
across GPU models only through the random stream. At v4's final checkpoint,
each arm's mean reward varies across seeds by at most \vSeedRangeMax{} points
(standard deviation at most \vSeedSDmax). For comparison, re-running a
$10$-sentence development evaluation moved its score by $1.1$ points.
\textbf{Confidence intervals} are $95\%$ percentile bootstraps over test
sentences ($2{,}000$ resamples). The same resampled sentences are used for
every arm, so differences between arms are \emph{paired}. Resampling
seed-averaged per-sentence values captures sentence-to-sentence variation as
well as the residual randomness of prompts and task-model samples, because the
latter is independent per sentence.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Besides the curve itself, we summarize each arm by:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{hypervolume}: the area dominated by the $20$ points in the
        (content, sentiment) plane, with reference point $(0,0)$, as a
        percentage of the $100\times100$ box. This is the standard scalar
        summary of a tradeoff curve, and it depends on neither reward;
  \item \emph{calibration}: the mean of $|\mathbb{E}[c\mid\tau] - \tau|$, and
        the mean shortfall $(\tau - \mathbb{E}[c\mid\tau])_+$, over the
        \emph{binding} floors $\tau = 30$--$90$. These lie above every arm's
        unconstrained content and below the copy prompts' ceiling;
  \item \emph{distinct points}: the number of floors whose point does not
        coincide, within $2$ points, with the previous floor's. This is a
        direct measure of clustering;
  \item \emph{prompts per $\tau$}: the median, over the grid, of the number of
        distinct prompts the policy emits across the $500$ test sentences;
  \item \emph{per-sample reward}: Reward~I averaged over sentences and floors,
        the training objective of v4. For v5 it is reported for reference only.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Test-split learning curves.}
Each arm's checkpoints every $1{,}500$ steps are evaluated with the same
protocol (one seed), giving matched test-split learning curves.

\paragraph{Supporting campaign (v3): training-seed replication on a small dev set.}
The earlier campaign trained five arms $\times$ three seeds with the same code
and budget. Its in-training evaluation ran on the \textbf{development split
capped at $10$ sentences}, a speed hack in the data loader, and its checkpoints
were later lost to a filesystem purge. Its scores survive only as the
experiment tracker's scalar history (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}). These are
$10$-sentence dev numbers, not test numbers. We use them only for what v4 and v5
cannot provide, namely the spread across \emph{training} seeds.

\paragraph{Compute.}
Each run occupies one GPU ($\ge\!40$\,GB, natively bf16) and runs as a chain of
$4$-hour scheduler cycles, each resuming from the most recent checkpoint. Step
time is $17$\,s on an H100 and $27$--$44$\,s on L40S/A100/RTX~A6000.
Generation by the frozen task model accounts for $97.6\%$ of step time; content
and sentiment scoring together take $2.3\%$, and the policy update under
$0.4\%$. A full $20$-point test evaluation takes $10$--$40$ minutes depending on
the GPU.

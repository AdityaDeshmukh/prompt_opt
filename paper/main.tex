\author{Aditya Deshmukh \and Lav R. Varshney}

\begin{abstract}
We study prompt optimization for a multi-objective text task, where a single
policy must trace a whole tradeoff curve rather than reach one operating point.
A $\lmb$-conditioned policy emits a short discrete prompt that steers a
\emph{frozen} task language model. $\lmb$ sets a floor on content
preservation, and sweeping it traces a curve of expected content against
expected sentiment. We first show that the natural reward for this setting, a
floor applied to every sampled rewrite, does not target that curve. It rewards
sentiment weighted by the probability of meeting the floor, so its switch
points between prompts are set by how each prompt's per-sample content is
spread, not by where its mean lies. Combined with a policy that settles on a few
prompts, it maps many floors to one point, far from the line
$\mathbb{E}[c] = \tau$. We replace it with a floor on the policy's
\emph{expected} content, imposed through a quadratic penalty whose gradient is a
per-rollout Lagrangian reward with a multiplier estimated from each group of
samples. We train with \rrebel{}, which regresses the $\beta$-scaled log-ratio
difference of two sampled prompts onto their reward difference under a robust
discrepancy, with rewards standardized within each group, and develop its
theory in the style of DPO. For any discrepancy and any full-support sampler,
the population minimizer is a KL-regularized optimum. Standardization gives
every context the same trust-region radius. Pairwise differencing makes robust
discrepancies unbiased. KL-free GRPO is \rrebel{}-$\ell_2$-std with lag one.
Under the new reward, the fixed point meets every binding floor in expectation,
up to the frontier's slope divided by the penalty weight. On the
$\wNsent$-sentence Yelp test split, \rrebel{} with a Huber discrepancy has the
best curve: hypervolume $\wHuberHV$ against $\wGentHV$ for the strongest GRPO
baseline, with a paired $95\%$ interval of $[\wGapHuberGentHVLo,
\wGapHuberGentHVHi]$ for the difference. Its expected content stays within
$\wHuberCalib$ points of the floor on average, and its $\wNlam$ floors give
$\wHuberDistinct$ distinct points, against $\vHuberDistinct$ under the
per-sample floor. The expectation floor has a cost, which we measure directly.
At the middle floors the learned prompts meet it largely by making the task
model either copy the input or write a short, unrelated positive sentence.
The share of rewrites that are positive and meet their floor falls from
$\exFourHuberSuccess\%$ to $\exFiveHuberSuccess\%$. An expected-value tradeoff
curve can be optimized exactly, but it is the wrong target when every
individual rewrite should be a faithful transfer.
\end{abstract}
